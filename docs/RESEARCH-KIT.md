# ProCircuit player research kit

Version 0.2, 13 September 2026. Owner: Manu Dubey. Change from 0.1: thirteen changes from the synthetic dry run (SYNTHETIC-DRY-RUN.md section 5) and the decision that WTA players and doubles are in Release 1 (register A21). This is the document to take into the room (or onto the video call) for eight to ten discovery and usability sessions with real players before committing to build ProCircuit. It assumes the reader has not read the PRDs: everything a session needs is written out here in full, including the questions, spoken as they should be asked.

Two prototypes are the research instrument, with a third on the way. The main one (`procircuit-dashboard.html`) shows Arya Dubey, a fictional Stage 2 player at ATP #487. The second (`procircuit-dashboard-neumayer.html`) shows Lukas Neumayer, a real Stage 3 player at ATP #227, with a fully loaded season. The third (`procircuit-dashboard-berger.html`) shows Sofia Berger, a fictional WTA Stage 2 player at #512 in singles and #276 in doubles, and is the file for every WTA participant. Both existing files work on a laptop or a phone at 390px wide, so a session can run on whatever device the player already has in their hand.

Stimulus rule. Record, before the session, which build the participant will see and how far it is from them: tour (ATP or WTA), stage, home currency (the prototype prices everything in A$ and shows an Austrian player's routes), doubles. Show Arya to a Stage 1 or Stage 2 ATP player, Neumayer to a Stage 3 ATP player, Berger to any WTA player. Where no build fits (a Stage 1 player, a Stage 3 WTA player, a doubles-first player, anyone whose home currency is not AUD, USD or CNY), say so to the participant in one sentence before task 1 ("this is built around a different player; tell me every time it isn't you") and mark the session as mismatch data in the scoring sheet so those answers are read for what they say about the gap, not about the feature. In the dry run, six of eight sessions opened with "this is not me" and it coloured everything after.

## 1. Who to recruit and how

ProCircuit's own logic splits players into three ranking stages, and the research sample should follow the same split, because the whole premise of the product is that a player at #620 and a player at #190 are running different businesses. Recruit across all three stages rather than clustering around whichever players are easiest to reach, and across three further splits the stage quota is blind to: tour (at least three of the eight to ten should be WTA players, since WTA is in Release 1), doubles (at least one player whose ranking or income is doubles-first, since the product shows a doubles ranking and counts doubles prize money but models no draws or partners), and who the buyer is (at least two players whose money is held by a parent, partner or coach, because the dry run suggested those players disown the runway number and are a different customer). Minors: Stage 1 runs close to 18; recruit only players 18 or over for this round, and if a 16 or 17 year old is the right participant, obtain a parent's written consent, have the parent present for the consent script, and apply the health-information rule below to anything said.

| Stage | Definition | Target sessions | Why they will answer differently |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1, Building | No ATP points, or ATP above roughly 800; ITF-led | 2 | Almost no discretionary income, often no coach at all, most price-sensitive on both the subscription and the idea of a platform fee on patron money, and the most likely to say a paid tool is a luxury they cannot justify yet |
| 2, Emerging | ATP roughly 450 to 800 | 4 | The band the product is built around: real travel costs, no team, the most acute need for a runway number and a cost-to-prize decision, and the group most likely to already be doing this badly on a phone notes app or a spreadsheet |
| 3, Established | ATP inside roughly 450, especially those just outside the top 200 | 3 to 4 | More likely to have a coach, a physio or a stringer already, more prize money but also more points to defend and more travel complexity, and more likely to already have an informal support network (family, a small sponsor, a federation grant) that changes how a patron programme and a subscription price both land |

A player just outside the top 200 already sees the ATP tour's infrastructure and may have someone doing parts of this job informally (a parent managing bookings, a manager taking a cut, a federation covering some travel), so the useful finding there is less "would you use this" and more "which parts would you hand over and which you would keep doing yourself." A player around #600 typically has none of that scaffolding and is more likely to treat the runway number and the patron programme as the reason to open the app at all, not a nice extra. Recruiting only from one end will make the product look either too basic or too advanced; only a mixed sample shows why.

Where to find them: tournament weeks are the highest-yield source, since players in this band spend long stretches in a hotel lobby or a players' lounge waiting on a match, a draw or a delayed flight, and a 60-minute conversation is often a welcome break. ITF and Challenger qualifying weeks put a wide ranking spread in one place. Touring and club coaches are a second channel: a coach with three or four players in this band can introduce all of them at once and lends credibility a cold approach cannot. National federations (Austrian, given the Neumayer prototype, but any federation running a pathway programme) often keep a list of pathway players and can make a warm introduction. Stringers and physios who travel the circuit know almost everyone in a 200-place band and are underused as a route in. Player WhatsApp groups, used for hotel shares, practice partners and string swaps, reach players invisible to any official list; a message from a player already in the group, not from Manu directly, will outperform a cold post.

An incentive should be offered, since this is real time taken from a working week, but sized and framed so it does not tilt the answers. Cash, paid at the end regardless of what was said, in the order of A$100 to A$150 (roughly a restring plus a training session, a scale players in this band understand without conversion), is cleanest: disclosed upfront, not conditional on liking the product or signing up, and the same for everyone. A voucher tied to something the product touches (a stringing credit, a hotel credit) should be avoided, since it quietly primes a favourable view of the exact category being tested.

Some of the eight to ten players approached will already be, or will become mid-session, real prospective customers, and that needs a clear rule. During the session, Manu is a researcher, not a salesperson: no pitching, no "this is why you'd want to upgrade," no promising a launch date or a discount for being first. If a player asks to sign up at the end, take their name and email, say plainly the product is not built yet, and write the request down as a signal of intent rather than closing anything in the room. Watch for courtesy answers from anyone unusually enthusiastic or already known to Manu, and deliberately include one or two participants with no prior connection at all, found through a federation list or a stringer's introduction, as a check against a sample that is quietly all friends.

## 2. Consent and ethics

Say the following, or something very close to it, before recording starts: "I'm going to record this, audio and the screen, so I can listen back properly instead of trying to write everything down while we talk. Only Manu and the small team building this will ever see or hear it. It won't be posted anywhere, and if anything you say ends up in a summary for someone else, like an investor or a future teammate, it will be a general point with your name and any identifying detail removed, not a quote with your name on it. You can stop at any point, skip a question, or ask me to stop recording, and none of that affects the A$[amount] either way. Is that alright with you?" Wait for a clear yes before continuing, and note the time consent was given.

This domain will surface real financial distress and real mental health material, not hypothetical versions of it, because the questions deliberately ask about runway, about who a player tells after a bad loss, and about whether they would ever say difficult things out loud to software. Some players will say more than the question asked for. The right response is to let it be said, not to steer away from it, not to offer advice or reassurance that oversteps what a researcher can responsibly give, and not to diagnose or label what is being described. If a genuine safety concern comes up, rather than ordinary frustration, name a real resource once, clearly and without making a scene of it (a national federation's player welfare contact, or a players' assistance body such as the ITF's own support channels, if the player is affiliated with one), then let the player decide whether to say more. This is the posture the product itself is required to take: the Mindset Coach never diagnoses, never uses clinical language, and shows a "someone to call" card whenever a note matches a distress pattern, a card that cannot be switched off. The research session should hold itself to the same standard, since the player is trusting a person in the room the same way they would be asked to trust the software.

Do not promise, in the session or after: that the product will be built as shown, that a specific price is final, that a specific feature will ship, that their feedback decides whether ProCircuit exists, or that anything disclosed is confidential from investors and future teammates in an unlimited sense (it is confidential from becoming identifiable, not confidential from being used at all, and that distinction should be stated plainly if asked). This mirrors the real-person governance already set for the prototype's own demo players: only public facts appear about a named real player, anything private is invented and labelled illustrative, and no fabricated activity is attributed to a real person. Apply that rule in reverse to participants: nothing a real player discloses, especially about money or mental state, should ever appear in a deck, a demo, or a future PRD in a form specific enough that the player or someone who knows them could recognise it. A composite ("several Stage 2 players described not trusting a cost estimate they hadn't seen the flight prices behind") is fine. A named or recognisable anecdote about a real player's finances or mental health is not, regardless of how illustrative or flattering it is meant to be.

## 3. The 70-minute session

The order matters more than it looks like it should. The prototype must not be shown until the unprompted discovery block is finished, because the moment a player sees a runway number on a screen, every answer about how they currently think about money will be contaminated by what they just saw. Keep a phone or a laptop timer visible to yourself, not to the player, so the session does not run long into their day.

Minutes 0 to 5, welcome and consent. Small talk about their week, their last result if it is public knowledge, and the travel they have coming up, both to build rapport and because the answers to those questions are themselves useful context for reading everything that follows. Deliver the consent script from section 2 and get a clear yes before recording.

Minutes 5 to 18, unprompted discovery. Nothing on a screen yet. Ask these questions in order, in this wording:

"Walk me through how you actually decided which tournament you're playing three weeks from now. What did you look at, and who did you talk to before you booked anything?" Probe: "What made you rule other weeks out?" and "Was any of that written down anywhere, or was it all in your head?"

"Think about the last flight or hotel you paid for. What happened to that receipt?" If the answer is that someone else paid, branch: "Who paid, and do they know how many weeks the money would last?" Otherwise probe: "Do you know, right now, off the top of your head, how many weeks your savings would cover if you didn't win another cheque?" Leave silence here. Do not fill the gap while they do the mental arithmetic, and do not help if they say they don't know; that itself is the finding. If they answer with a number straight away, add: "If an app worked that out from your receipts and got a different number, which would you believe?"

"Tell me about the last time you lost a match that really got to you, one you were expected to win or one that just hurt. Who did you actually tell, and what did you say to them?" Probe: "Did you write anything down about it, to yourself or to anyone?" Leave silence after the first answer before probing; players often add the real answer on the second breath, not the first.

"Is there anyone, family, fans, a small sponsor, who sends you money or covers costs for you regularly? How did that arrangement start, and does anyone else know about it?"

"Tell me about the last time the weather or the court surface changed how you thought about your racquet or your strings." Probe: "Who did you talk to about that, if anyone?"

Minutes 18 to 47, guided tasks. Hand the player the device with the appropriate prototype already open (per the stimulus rule) and work through the task list in section 4, reading each instruction exactly as written. In the dry run every session ran over by one to four minutes and the overrun always came out of pricing, so the session is now 70 minutes with the extra time here and in pricing, and task 4 (the conditions brief) is the one to drop first if the clock demands it, since it was the cleanest confirmation of the eight.

Minutes 47 to 62, pricing. Use the section 5 script rather than asking directly whether they would pay.

Minutes 62 to 70, close. Ask: "Is there anything on that screen that surprised you, in either direction?" and "If you could keep exactly one thing you saw today and throw away everything else, what would you keep?" Thank them, hand over the incentive, and if they ask about signing up, follow the rule in section 1.

## 4. Prototype tasks

Read each instruction aloud exactly as written, so every session gets the same stimulus. Give the player the device and do not narrate or explain the interface before they have tried it themselves; the confusion is the data. Do not explain, even if asked: in the dry run the moderator broke this rule at least once in every session, most often on tasks 2, 3 and 6 (explaining where a cost figure or the A$60 constant came from, saying "it's just a draft" before the approve tap, explaining the runway tile before it was read), and each time the answer that followed was worth less. When a player asks a question the task is meant to surface, reflect it back ("what would you expect it to be?") and write the question down. One scripted exception: if a player asks where the audio goes, say "it's transcribed and then deleted within seven days; the transcript stays" and nothing more, because the question is a finding and a reassurance closes it.

| # | Read aloud | What success looks like | Watch for | What it tests |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hand over the device and say nothing for ten seconds; record the first thing they touch or look at. Then: "This is what you'd see if you opened the app first thing in the morning. Don't tap anything else yet: just tell me what these three boxes are telling you, and what you'd do next." | The player names the runway figure, the decision due, and the patron movement, roughly in that order, without being walked through it, and lands on the decision card as the next thing they'd act on | Whether they reach for the ranking chart at the top instead, whether the order feels backwards to them, whether one of the three tiles means nothing to them at a glance | Whether runway, decision due and patron movement are the right three answers, in that order (the product's central premise) |
| 2 | "Here's this week's tournament. Imagine the flights were from your own airport and the hotel was the one you'd usually book. Would you enter it? Talk me through what you're actually checking before you'd decide." For a Stage 3 player add: "Is this the kind of week you'd be looking at, or would you be higher up the ladder?" | The player engages with the cost breakdown and the ranking, and either says they'd trust it or names specifically what they'd want to see before they would (real flight prices, their own past hotel costs, whether the entry is guaranteed or a qualifying spot) | Whether they even notice the ranking logic exists, whether they mentally override it and on what basis, whether they ask where the prize estimate came from | The cost-to-prize ranking and its A$60-per-point assumption: whether a player would actually trust a machine-ranked shortlist with their entry money, or what they would insist on checking themselves |
| 3 | "Imagine you just walked off court after today's loss. Say out loud, or type if you'd rather, what you'd actually put into this note." If the player hesitates, offer to step out of the room for a minute while they record, and note whether the note changes when nobody is listening. | The player either attempts a real note in their own words, or explicitly declines and says why | Hesitation before starting, what gets left out compared with what they said earlier in discovery about who they tell after a loss, whether the mood and tag fields feel intrusive or beside the point | Whether a player would ever say financially or emotionally real things out loud to software, and whether the capture experience matches how they actually process a loss |
| 4 | "Here's what the app thinks about the conditions for this event, including a suggested string tension test. Would you actually act on this? What would you do with it?" | The player reads the tension test and either says they'd try it, or says they'd run it past their stringer or coach first, and can restate in their own words that the app is only proposing, not deciding | Whether the sentence about the stringer deciding lands as reassuring or as the app trying to do a stringer's job, whether the temperature and altitude framing matches how they actually think about conditions | The stringer boundary line and whether the underlying test thresholds (tension step, altitude, temperature and humidity cutoffs) match a real player's own judgement |
| 5 | "Imagine you have no supporters signed up at all today. Would you build this page? What would you actually charge, and who's the first person you'd ask?" | The player engages with the tiers and either names real people they'd approach or explicitly says they wouldn't ask anyone, and can articulate why | Discomfort, embarrassment, or a flat refusal versus genuine interest; whether they'd use their real name and photo publicly; what they think is a fair or an awkward price to ask for | Whether players would actually ask people for money through this, and how the ask feels rather than whether the mechanics work |
| 6 | "Go ahead and approve this. Before you tap it, tell me what you think is about to happen. Now tap it. What actually happened?" | The player reads the one-line consequence sentence next to the button before tapping and predicts what it says. Note: on the dashboard card the sentence says the update is sent to patrons on this tap (a deliberate one-tap decision, A7); on the Content Agent page the tap opens a confirm step. Score on whether the sentence was read and believed, not on whether the label "Approve & publish" made the player expect a send, since in the dry run the label did that in eight of eight sessions. Run it on the Content Agent page, then show the dashboard card and ask the same question again. | Whether the line was read at all, whether "publish" alone drove the prediction, whether the player looks for an undo, and whether being wrong once makes them read the line the second time | Whether the one-sentence consequence line is doing its job on both surfaces, and whether the button label should change ("Approve draft" was suggested twice in the dry run) |
| 7 | "This is what you'd see on the free version of this page. What do you think you're looking at, and would this make you want to pay, or put you off?" | The player correctly identifies it as a preview of something locked, and gives a genuine reaction rather than confusion about whether it's broken | Whether they think the page is broken or empty rather than deliberately locked, whether the single upgrade action feels fair or feels like a trick | Whether a locked, dimmed real surface is the right way to show a free player what they're missing, and whether the free tier should show this much at all |

## 5. Pricing and willingness to pay

Asking "would you pay A$49 a month for this" is close to useless in this population: prize money arrives in irregular lumps rather than a salary, and a meaningful share of players in this ranking band are still partly supported by a parent or a small sponsor, so the honest answer to a direct question is often "it depends whose money it is and what month you're asking about," which is true but not actionable on its own. Ask around the price rather than at it.

If the player said in discovery that a parent, partner or coach holds the money, ask this first: "Whose money would a subscription actually come out of, and would they be the one deciding?" Then continue with their own numbers, since a price only means something against a real budget: "Roughly, what did stringing, hotels and flights cost you last month, all in?" and "Think about your worst month financially this year: could you have found A$49 that month, and where would it have come from?" Convert the prices into the player's own currency before the session (write both on the card you read from) and anchor them to the player's own answers, not to a fixed restring or hotel night, since in the dry run the restring anchor failed for self-stringers and for a Stage 3 player stringing four frames a week, and the hotel anchor was corrected by six of eight: "This would run about A$49 a month, which is about [their currency], roughly [what they said a restring costs them]; or A$149 a month, about [their currency], roughly [what they said a night costs them]. Which of those, if either, feels like something you'd find room for, and which feels like it's asking too much?" Leave silence after this question rather than offering a third option immediately. For everyone else, follow with "Whose money would that actually come out of: yours, a parent's, a sponsor's, or somewhere else?" since the answer changes who the real buyer is, not just the price they'll tolerate.

Ask about the free trial separately, since a fourteen-day no-card trial changes the decision from "would you pay" to "would you start": "If you could try all of this for two weeks without putting a card in, what would make you decide to keep it after that, and what would make you cancel?"

The platform fee on patron income needs its own question, because it is not a subscription cost, it is a cut taken from money the player has just raised from people who support them personally, and players are likely to feel that differently. Ask: "Say your supporters gave you A$600 this month. How would you feel about A$48 of that going to the platform before it reaches you, versus A$30?" (testing the 8 percent Pro rate against the 5 percent Elite rate; both rates are settled as of 13 September and taken on the gross with Stripe's own charge shown separately, and the prototype's payout table now shows exactly that, so the screen can stay visible for this question). Probe: "Does that feel like a bank fee, like a manager's cut, or like something else, and does it change whether you'd bother building the page at all?"

## 6. What to watch for rather than ask

Some of the most useful signal in these sessions will never be said out loud. Watch for hesitation before any tap that commits to something, most of all "accept entry" and "send": a pause there usually means the player does not yet believe what the screen just told them. Watch for re-reading: a player who reads the cost breakdown or the tension test twice is telling you the first pass didn't land, even if they sound confident afterwards. Note the very first thing a player touches on the dashboard, unprompted, since that is a truer statement of what they think matters than anything said when asked directly. Note what a player skips over entirely, a chart, a field, a whole card, since silence about a feature the product's owner considers important is as informative as a complaint. Watch for a player looking for something that isn't there: a way to message their coach, a calendar sync, an undo button, or a chart they expect to be tappable. Each of those is a feature request nobody had to ask for.

## 7. Capturing and analysing the sessions

Use a simple note-taking template during and immediately after each session, with one row per notable moment: the task or question number, the time stamp, the player's words as close to verbatim as can be managed, the behaviour observed alongside the words (hesitation, a tap, a laugh, a long pause), a rough sentiment mark, and a flag for whether the moment confirms or contradicts a specific assumption from the PRDs or the review register, named by its item where one exists (for example "A1," "D: A$60/point," "M-GATE-1"). Write this up within a day of the session, while the moment is still recoverable in detail; a week later, only the summary survives and the texture that actually changes a requirement is gone. Treat anything a player says about an injury, a surgery, medication or their mental state as health information: record it as a general observation ("reluctant to put anything about the body into a note"), never as the specific fact, and never in a quote that could identify them.

Eight or ten sessions read individually are eight or ten anecdotes; a scoring sheet is what turns them into something comparable. Build one sheet with a row per task or pricing question and one column per participant, each cell scored on a short scale decided before the first session runs, not invented afterwards to fit what was heard. A workable scale for most rows is a three-point one: confirms the assumption, partially confirms or overrides part of it, or contradicts it, with a short quote underneath each cell as the evidence. Tag every participant's column with their stage (1, 2 or 3), tour (ATP or WTA), whether they are doubles-first, who holds their money, and whether the stimulus fitted them, so the sheet can be read two ways: down a column for one player's whole session, and across a row to see whether Stage 2 and Stage 3 players actually answered the same task differently, which the sampling in section 1 was built to reveal.

| Row | What it scores | Read across the row for |
|---|---|---|
| Three answers, right order | Named runway, decision, patrons in that order unprompted | Whether the order is universal or stage-dependent |
| Trusts the cost-to-prize ranking | Trusted it outright, trusted it with named caveats, didn't trust it | Whether trust tracks with how established the player's own process already is |
| Would say the match note aloud | Did it naturally, did it with editing or omission, declined | Whether Stage 1 players (less used to any support staff) are more or less guarded than Stage 3 |
| Tension test reads correctly | Understood it as a proposal, was unsure who decides, thought the app was deciding | Whether the stringer-boundary sentence needs to be stronger or clearer |
| Would build the patron page | Named real people to ask, hesitant, refused | Whether reluctance clusters by stage, nationality, or something else entirely |
| Understood the approval gate | Correctly predicted nothing was sent, was unsure, was surprised | Whether the one-line consequence text is doing its job |
| A$49 reaction | Comfortable, tight but doable, too much | Whose money it would come from, by stage |
| A$149 reaction | Comfortable, tight but doable, too much | Same, and whether Elite features justify the jump for anyone |
| 8 percent patron fee reaction | Acceptable, resented, would avoid the programme because of it | Whether the fee changes whether they'd even try the patron page |
| First unprompted touch | Ranking chart, a tile, the decision card, something else | Whether the product's premise about what matters first survives contact |
| Stimulus fit | Fitted, partly (currency or stage), did not (tour, doubles, buyer) | Which answers to read as gap data rather than feature data |

Coding beyond the sheet should stay light: group verbatim quotes under a small number of named themes as they recur (for example "wants to see the flight price behind the estimate," "would ask a parent before a stranger," "already keeps a mental runway number and doesn't trust anyone else's"), and only promote a theme to a finding once it appears across at least six of the eight to ten sessions, or as a clean split by stage that is itself the finding (if every Stage 3 player trusts the ranking and no Stage 1 player does, that split is the result, not an average to smooth over). One or two dissenting players are worth quoting as a named edge case, but should not overturn a pattern six or more agreed on.

Write the result up so it changes the documents, not so it sits in a folder. For each finding, name the exact requirement or register item it bears on and state plainly whether it is confirmed, needs a specific change, or is still unresolved and why. The natural home for this is the review register itself: add a column to PRD-REVIEW-REGISTER.md next to each decision-needed row and each Section D placeholder recording what the research showed and the session count behind it, so the register becomes the place where the research and the requirements meet rather than a separate report nobody reopens.

## 8. Where this research settles specific decisions

The synthetic dry run produced ten hypotheses (SYNTHETIC-DRY-RUN.md section 6); the real sessions should be read against them, and each is tagged there with the register item or PRD it would change. The strongest were about the button label on task 6, the fare and home airport behind the shortlist, the runway tile being disowned by players who do not hold their own money, the A$149 price finding no individual buyer, and the free tier being enough for team-supported and parent-managed players.

Several items already flagged as open in the review register can be answered by these sessions rather than by guesswork. The cost-to-prize ranking and its A$60-per-point placeholder (Section D, PRD-01) is tested by task 2 and is the single biggest untested assumption in the product: if players consistently override the ranking or refuse to trust it without seeing the flights behind it, the requirement needs a visible source for the estimate, not just a number. The patron platform fee, 8 percent or 10 percent and gross or net of Stripe's own charges (A1, A2), is tested by the pricing script in section 5 and by task 5's read on whether the fee changes willingness to build the page at all. The tension test's underlying constants (the one-kilogram step, the frames ladder, the 400-metre altitude threshold, the temperature and humidity cutoffs, Section D) are tested by task 4, alongside whether a stringer share scope belongs in Release 1 (Section E). Whether the Free tier should show a read-only shortlist, both, or neither (A15) is tested by task 7. Whether the approval gate's one-sentence consequence line is enough, and whether one-tap publishing from a dashboard card needs the same two-step confirmation as the Content Agent page (A7), is tested by task 6. The A$49 and A$149 price points, while not a register item, are the most consequential untested number in the whole plan and the reason the pricing section exists.

## 9. Risks in this research design

The most dangerous risk is the plainest one: a founder interviewing his own prospective customers tends to hear what he wants to hear, both in the room and later when writing it up. The mitigations are structural, not a matter of trying harder to be objective. Decide the scoring categories in section 7 before the first session, not after, so a surprising answer cannot be quietly reclassified to fit an expected pattern. Where possible, have a second person sit in and score independently, comparing sheets afterwards rather than discussing impressions during the session. Record everything and re-code from the recording rather than from memory a week later, when memory has already smoothed the awkward parts into something more flattering. Recruit one or two participants with no prior connection to Manu at all, through a federation or a stringer, as a check against a sample that is quietly all friends and therefore quietly all agreeable.

A second risk is sample size and shape: eight to ten players, however carefully split across stages, is not a statistically representative sample of a global tour, and a pattern seen in six of eight sessions is a strong signal to act on, not proof. The stage quota in section 1 shows whether an answer is universal or stage-dependent, but it cannot promise the two or three players in each cell are typical of their stage rather than unusual within it; treat every finding as a strong hypothesis for the next round, not a settled fact.

A third risk is anchoring in the reverse direction from the one this kit already guards against: the unprompted discovery block can still leak intent if the session is booked as being about "a financial planning tool for tennis players," since the player will already be thinking in those terms before the call begins. Describe it neutrally when booking, as being about how players currently manage the business side of their career, without naming ProCircuit's features, so the discovery block in minutes 5 to 18 is genuinely unprimed.

A fourth risk is the one this kit spends the most words on for good reason: real financial and mental health disclosure carries a duty of care that a product research session is not resourced to fully discharge. The consent script, the resource-naming protocol and the anonymisation rule in section 2 are the mitigation, and none should be rushed to keep a session on schedule; a session that runs over because a player needed to be heard properly has done its job better than one that hit every timing mark.

A fifth, smaller risk is courtesy bias specific to pricing: a player interviewed by the person building the product, just after being treated generously, is inclined to be agreeable about price in a way they never would be with their own money on the line later. The behavioural framing in section 5, anchoring the price against a real recent expense and asking whose money it would come from, exists to make the question harder to answer politely.
